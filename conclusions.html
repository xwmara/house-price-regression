Conclusions

What drives house prices in Ames

The multiple regression of log price explains 85.1% of the variation in prices in the training set, against 50.1% for living area alone. Holding the other characteristics constant:

  • Quality is the variable most correlated with price (0.80), and each step up the 10-level overall quality scale adds about 11.3% to the price.
  • Size raises the price less than proportionally, so the price per square foot falls as houses get larger: a 10% larger house sells for about 4.4% more (elasticity 0.45), and a 10% larger lot for about 1.0% more.
  • Age and garage: each decade of newer construction adds about 2.5%, and each extra garage space about 5.7%.
  • Location still matters after all of these: an otherwise identical house costs about 14% more in Crawford and 17% less in Iowa DOT and Rail Road than in North Ames.
  • Room counts add little once living area is known. Bathrooms, bedrooms and total rooms have small, mostly insignificant effects with robust standard errors, and Lasso drops all three in its simpler version. For the same living area, more rooms just means smaller rooms.

These are associations estimated from observed sales, not causal effects: for example, the quality coefficient also picks up features that come with higher-quality houses but are not in the model.

How well the model predicts

On 881 test houses that were not used for estimation:

Model Mean absolute error Mean absolute % error R² (USD)
Baseline: training average $57,283 35.8% 0.0%
Simple regression (living area) $37,722 22.0% 49.6%
Multiple log regression $18,700 10.8% 87.3%
  • The multiple log regression halves the error of the simple model, and its test errors are no larger than its training errors, so it does not overfit.
  • Converting log predictions back to dollars needs a correction: without Duan’s smearing factor, predictions are too low by $3,739 on average.
  • Ridge and Lasso do not improve on OLS (best test RMSE $28,221 for Lasso against $28,079 for OLS). With 2,049 houses for 32 predictors and moderate multicollinearity, OLS is already stable. A simpler Lasso with 11 predictors loses only a little accuracy (MAPE 11.6%).

Where the model is weaker

  • Both ends of the market. The percentage error is highest for the cheapest quarter of houses (14.4%), where unusual sales such as foreclosures and family sales are about 3 times as common as in the most expensive quarter. The dollar error is highest for the most expensive quarter ($30,303), which the model underpredicts by $17,576 on average.
  • Single houses. The model is accurate on average, but a 95% prediction interval for one house runs from about 28% below to 31% above the predicted median price. Intervals built from the empirical residual distribution cover 95.5% of test prices, closer to the nominal 95% than the normal intervals (96.9%).

Possible uses in valuation and credit risk

A model of this kind is the core of an automated valuation model (AVM), which lenders use to check the value of a property offered as collateral:

  • Collateral valuation. A predicted price with an interval gives a quick, consistent first estimate of a property’s value, and the interval width shows how much to rely on it.
  • Flagging unusual transactions. Sales far below the predicted price were mostly not normal sales: abnormal sales such as foreclosures, sales between family members, or partial sales of unfinished houses. A large gap between the agreed price and the model’s estimate is a signal worth reviewing, for example for loan-to-value checks.
  • Segment-aware caution. The model is most reliable for mid-priced houses; estimates for the cheapest and the most expensive properties need wider margins or manual review.

Limitations

  • One town, five years. The data cover Ames, Iowa, from 2006 to 2010, a period that includes the 2008 financial crisis. Prices are nominal and the model has no time effects, so it should not be applied to other markets or to current prices.
  • Nine predictors. Many available characteristics, such as the basement, the condition of the house or the sale type, are not used, which may explain part of the larger errors at the top of the market.
  • Functional form. The RESET test indicates that some non-linear effects or interactions are missing.
  • Ordinal quality as a number. Overall quality enters as a 1-10 score, which assumes equal steps between levels.
  • Grouped neighborhoods. 4 neighborhoods with fewer than 10 training sales share one “Other” coefficient.

Possible extensions

  • Add the year and condition of sale, so that time effects and non-market sales are modeled instead of left in the errors.
  • Allow non-linear effects, for example splines for age and size, and interactions between size and neighborhood.
  • Use the coordinates of the houses for a spatial model of location.
  • Compare with tree-based models, such as random forests or gradient boosting, using the same train/test split and dollar metrics.
